    How the grid spins

    One giant wheel

    Every big power station on a grid spins in lockstep: the turbines and generators in a gas, nuclear or hydro plant all turn at exactly the same rhythm. Together they behave like one huge shared wheel. Its speed is the grid frequency: 50 turns per second (50 Hz) across Switzerland and continental Europe, 60 Hz in most of the Americas.

    Generators push the rim forward. Every motor, heater and phone charger drags on it. If pushes and drags are equal, the wheel keeps its pace. If demand wins, it slows down; if supply wins, it speeds up. That's the whole game.

    Try it

    Push or drag the wheel and watch the frequency. Then make it heavier.

    Inertia buys you seconds

    A heavy wheel is hard to slow down. The spinning steel in big generators stores a lot of energy, so when a power station suddenly trips, the frequency falls gently instead of crashing. That gives the automatic reserves time to react.

    Solar panels, wind farms and batteries connect through power electronics (inverters), so they add no spinning mass by default. A grid running mostly on wind and sun is a lighter wheel: it wobbles faster. In the game, watch the Inertia number fall when you replace gas with solar.

    Automatic reserves

    Grids don't rely on humans for the first seconds. Many plants carry primary control (in Europe called FCR): when the frequency drops, they automatically push a bit harder, and when it rises they ease off. In continental Europe these reserves are fully deployed at a deviation of 0.2 Hz, which is why the game's green band is 49.8 to 50.2 Hz.

    Primary control stops the fall but doesn't bring the wheel all the way back. Operators then dispatch more plants (that's you, tapping the spinners).

    Batteries and flywheels

    A BESS (battery energy storage system) can flip from charging to full discharge in a fraction of a second, so it's superb at frequency response. Its limit is energy: once empty it can't push any more. A flywheel is a literal spinning wheel you can bolt onto the grid: it soaks up spare energy by spinning faster and gives it back in milliseconds, and it adds real inertia, but it only holds a few seconds' worth.

    Not every plant is quick

    The mission

    Every grid starts with gas plants. Your job is to phase them out: build wind, solar, hydro and batteries, plus nuclear for base load where the country allows it, and switch the gas plants off and scrap them, without letting the frequency leave the band. The clean share counts the energy from hydro, nuclear, wind and solar over the last 24 game hours (batteries only shift energy, so they're left out). Each mission step pays a green subsidy, and the CO₂ price goes up every day, so gas gets more expensive the longer you wait. Run a full day without burning gas, with every fossil plant gone, and the mission is complete.

    New nuclear: France may build it. Switzerland keeps its existing plant, but new ones still await a public vote. Italy passed a law in 2026 to bring nuclear back with small modular reactors, not expected before the 2030s. Germany closed its last reactors in 2023.

    Two ways to look at it

    The default view is the spinning wheel. Switch on 3D world view in Settings to see the same grid as a little isometric world instead: every plant, battery and consumer is a small model, joined by neon wires to one central node, the grid, where you read the frequency. Energy pulses run along the wires (towards the grid from producers, out to consumers and charging batteries). The rules are exactly the same.

    When it goes wrong

    If the frequency keeps falling, grids protect themselves by switching off whole areas of customers on purpose (automatic load shedding), starting around 49 Hz in Europe. Too fast or too slow for too long, and generators disconnect to protect themselves, which makes things worse. That cascade is a blackout.

    The rules here

    • Keep the frequency in the green band (±0.2 Hz).
    • Outside the band an alarm counts down. Get back in time, or it's a blackout.
    • Hit ±1 Hz (49 Hz or 51 Hz) and it's an instant blackout.
    • Tap a generator on the wheel to switch it on or off. Long-press for details or to scrap it.
    • Build more with + on the cards. New assets arrive disconnected: tap them to connect them. Keep some idle as a reserve for emergencies.
    • Consumers pay for every MWh you deliver.
    • Prices are real-world ballparks (build cost, yearly upkeep, fuel and CO₂). To make that playable, money runs on a yearly clock: one game day books a full year of bills, fuel and upkeep.
    • Each new day brings more customers, wilder loads and more surprises.

    Game numbers are scaled so the action fits on a phone: real grids are far heavier and react over seconds, not this dramatically.
